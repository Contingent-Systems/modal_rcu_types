> All reviewers: Mechanization, and corrections to the published invariants


Since submission we have mechanized the global invariants, the reachability core
of the soundness argument, and every atomic action in full, in Rocq (about
23,100 lines and 1,116 lemmas and theorems, axiom-free).  The build refuses to
succeed if the development contains an admitted proof, or if any of its 328
assumption checks reports an axiom rather than closed under the
global context;
we mention this because a Rocq file that compiles proves considerably less than
it looks.  This turned up more than we expected, and we think it materially
strengthens the paper.

That target had the bug it exists to catch, and we report it because the
argument for having it is exactly what the bug illustrates.  A Print Assumptions
inside a Section prints nothing at all.  Five of ours were inside one -- both
read-side triples and the whole binary search tree chain -- so five results we
claimed were checked were not being checked.  They were sound, which we
confirmed by running the queries outside their sections, but the checking was
not.  They are moved out and the build now refuses an indented one.  The count
rose by five for that reason and no other.

Ten of the WellFormed invariants are wrong or missing:

- Unique-Reachable is vacuous as written, and admits heaps that are not trees.
- Readers-Iterators-Only is contradictory in any state containing both a reader
  and a freshly allocated node, so it can be used to derive anything.
- Fresh-Reachable is satisfied by its own hypothesis, and so constrains nothing.
- Fresh-Points-Iterator contradicts Fresh-Not-Reader.
- Unlinked-Reachability does not chain: its conclusion is weaker than its
  hypothesis, so the induction along a path stalls at the first freeable
  predecessor.
- Heap-Domain-Closure is false as stated.  It constrains every edge in the
  heap, but T-UnlinkH leaves the unlinked node still pointing at its old child
  and nothing orders the reclamation of two unlinked nodes, so a well-typed
  section may free a node that another, itself awaiting reclamation, still
  points at.  The pointer dangles harmlessly, in a node that is detached and
  unreachable -- which is exactly the exemption No-Sharing already carries and
  this one does not.
- Fresh-Not-Reader is too weak.  It denies a freshly allocated node the
  *iterator* and *unlinked* observations and says nothing about *freeable*, and
  that third case is the one T-Replace and T-Insert need.  Both link a fresh
  node under a reference the writer holds as an iterator; the write creates that
  edge, and Unlinked-Reachability then requires that if the new child is
  unlinked or freeable the parent is too -- which Writer-Unlink denies of an
  iterator.  So the obligation can be discharged only by denying both to the
  child, and the published invariant denies one.

  This one differs in kind from the others, and we state the difference rather
  than paper over it: the reachable states contain no such node, since a node
  becomes freeable only from unlinked, and the unlinked conjunct already denies
  that.  The property holds inductively over whole executions.  What fails is
  locality -- the action lemma is not provable from the invariants alone, which
  is the only thing a global invariant set is for.  Adding the conjunct restores
  it and costs nothing: SyncStop, the one action that could have been asked to
  pay for it, discharges the new conjunct from the old one.  We give a state
  satisfying the other eighteen invariants and the published form in which a
  node is both fresh and freeable, and a proof that linking it breaks
  Unlinked-Reachability.
- Unlinking is not said to be the writer's.  WriterFresh says an allocation
  belongs to the lock holder; nothing said the same of an unlinking, and the
  Unlinked-Reachability case of T-UnlinkH and T-Replace needs it.  That case
  ends at a detached predecessor of the node being unlinked -- No-Sharing
  supplies the detachment, the repaired premise of those rules excludes a
  rcuFresh one -- so the predecessor is unlinked or freeable by *some* thread,
  and Unlinked-Reachability's conclusion names the thread its hypothesis names.
  Like the previous item this is a failure of locality rather than a false
  statement, and it was found the same way, by attempting the case.  We give a
  state satisfying the other eighteen in which a node is unlinked by a thread
  that is not the lock holder.
- Iterator observations are not said to belong to an active thread, and this
  one is not merely a failure of locality.  SyncStart puts every unlinked node
  on the free list with the current readers as its bounding set, and
  the iterators-in-free-list invariant then demands that every thread observing
  such a node as an iterator is in that set: it is the obligation that a grace
  period waits for everyone who could still be looking.  Writer-Unlink rules out
  the writer.
  Nothing ruled out a thread that is neither the writer nor a current reader,
  and against such a thread the grace period would complete while a live
  reference remained -- which is the accident the whole structure exists to
  prevent.  The three together are a family: each kind of observation
  constrains who may hold it, WriterFresh does it for fresh, and the published
  set had exactly one of the three.  We give a state satisfying the other
  eighteen in which a node is observed as an iterator by a thread that is
  neither the writer nor a reader.
- Nothing says where free-list entries may live.  Three invariants mention the
  free list -- iterators-in-free-list, free-list-reachability and
  readers-in-free-list -- and all three are conditional on an entry existing;
  none says which nodes may have one.  Nothing in the published set therefore
  rules out an entry at a node that is not detached, not reachable, and not
  even in the heap.  SyncStart's specification says the grace period covers
  *exactly* the detached nodes, and re-establishing "exactly" needs to know
  that the entries already there were at detached nodes too; it is the one
  hypothesis of the snapshot step that no invariant supplies.  We give a state
  satisfying all nineteen with a free-list entry at a location carrying no
  observations at all.  The repair is a conjunct saying an entry implies the
  node is unlinked or freeable, which SyncStart establishes and which every
  other action either preserves trivially or -- Free and ReadEnd -- preserves
  because it only shrinks the free list.

Five of these are the consequential ones.  Unlinked-Reachability is discussed
under Delayed Ownership Transfer below; Heap-Domain-Closure is the invariant the
Free case rests on, and under the published statement that case cannot be proved
at all, which is presumably why our proof records it as trivial; and
Fresh-Not-Reader is what the two rules that link a fresh node need, so under the
published statement neither T-Replace nor T-Insert can be shown to preserve
Unlinked-Reachability; the missing statement about unlinking is what the two
rules that *unlink* a node need, for the same invariant; and the missing
statement about iterators is what SyncStart needs, without which a grace period
can complete too early.

Two further invariants are missing, and both were found by attempting the last of
the linking rules against the Iris invariant described below.  T-LinkF-Null
publishes a fresh node, and to do so it must know that no thread other than the
writer observes that node.  Two invariants ought to supply that between them and
neither does.

- Nothing says the root observation marks the root.  It is the one observation
  that carries no thread, and the published set constrains where every *tagged*
  observation may sit while saying nothing about this one, so a well-formed
  state may record it at a location that is not the root.  That also weakens
  Unique-Reachable-Tree's second part, whose conclusion is "iterator or root"
  and which is read as "every reachable node is the writer's, except the root
  itself" -- under the published set the exception is not tied to the root.
  This one is the only defect of the twenty that we later removed rather than
  repaired, and the reason is worth a sentence.  We first added it as a
  conjunct.  It is better read as an artefact of our encoding: we filed every
  observation under an observing thread, so the anonymous one had to be filed
  under some thread too, and three problems followed from that alone -- the
  conjunct had to be added, ReadEnd could not simply drop a departing thread's
  entries, and the clause relating a reader's entries to its registration had
  to be about tagged observations rather than about entries, which made it too
  weak to decide whether a reader has an entry at a node.  Reading the root
  observation off the root pointer instead removes all three at once.  The
  witness still stands -- the published nineteen really do not imply it -- but
  no action has to preserve it, and the reader's read closes as a consequence.
- WriterFresh is too weak.  It is stated with a stack reference in its
  hypothesis -- a thread that *names* a fresh node holds the lock -- so a fresh
  observation whose variable has left scope constrains nobody.  This corrects
  something we said above: the family of three is right, but the member the
  published set already had was the weak one, and it needs the same repair as
  the two we add.

One witness does for both: a state satisfying all nineteen in which the root
observation sits at a non-root location and a fresh observation belongs to a
thread that does not hold the lock.  The second repair is the obvious conjunct
and it is carried.  The first, as the bullet above says, we ended up removing
rather than carrying -- the encoding change makes it true by computation -- and
we report the witness because a reader of Unique-Reachable-Tree will assume the
property and the published set does not give it.

There is also a defect in the type rules rather than in an invariant.  Neither
T-Replace nor T-UnlinkH prevents a rcuFresh reference in the environment from
holding a field that points at the node the rule unlinks; the aliasing premises
quantify only over rcuItr references, and the denotation of a type environment
is an intersection rather than a separating conjunction, so nothing else
excludes it. The resulting state breaks Fresh-Points-Iterator and the denotation
of the fresh reference's own type, and linking that reference in afterwards
would splice a node already scheduled for reclamation back into the structure.
Both rules now carry a premise excluding it. Both worked examples still type
check unchanged, since the fresh node each builds points at the children of the
node being replaced rather than at that node.

A thirteenth invariant defect came out of the read side.  Exactly one of the
nineteen mentions the bounding set B -- RINFL says every thread in a free-list
entry is a bounding thread -- and nothing constrains B itself.  But SyncStop
blocks until B is empty, and the only action that removes a thread from it is
ReadEnd, which is a *reader's* action.  So a bounding thread that is not a
reader can never be removed and the grace period never completes; we give a
state satisfying all nineteen in which that happens.  The missing conjunct is
the one SyncStart in fact establishes, since it sets B to the current readers:
every bounding thread is a reader.  With it, the writer never bounds its own
grace period, which is what lets the thread running the wait finish it.  We
cannot prove liveness -- there is no operational semantics here -- but we can
prove the obstruction survives every step that could clear it, and we do: for
any sequence of ReadEnds by any readers in any order, the offending thread is
still bounding afterwards.

A fourteenth came out of the other end of the same exercise, and it is the
thirteenth's twin.  WUNLK says detaching observations are the writer's, but only
while a writer holds the lock; in an unlocked state it says nothing, so nothing
rules out a stray unlinked or freeable observation belonging to an arbitrary
thread.  We give a state satisfying all nineteen in which there is one.  That is
exactly what ReadBegin needs ruled out -- a thread entering a read critical
section while holding a detaching observation breaks RITR at once -- and the
unlocked state is precisely when a thread starts reading.  The repair is the one
the revision had already made to WFresh one invariant earlier: state it
unguarded.  It is true of every reachable state for the reason the rules already
record, since WriteEnd's own premise is that the critical section leaves nothing
detached.  That the same defect appears twice, one invariant apart, and that we
repaired the first without noticing the second, is the part worth reporting: the
guard is what makes each of them provable locally, and it is also what makes
them useless to every rule on the read side.

With both invariants in place, ReadBegin is a closed triple -- the first on the read side.
Its two premises, that the entering thread is not the writer and that it holds
no detaching observation, were hypotheses of the action lemma; they are now
proved, the first from the thread's own reader cell and the second from the two
unguarded invariants.

ReadEnd is the next rule, and it did not close for a long time.  The reason is
worth reporting in full, because what repaired it was a change to the model
rather than to the proof.  It is the one step in the system that writes state
another thread owns: the free-list snapshots belong to the writer, which
created them at SyncStart, and a departing reader rewrites every one it appears
in.  The obvious economy is to leave them alone and read a snapshot against the
threads still running, since a thread that has left its read section bounds
nothing; ReadEnd would then touch only its own reader cell.  We tried that and
it is unsound, because of re-entrancy -- a thread that leaves and enters a new
read section is a reader again, and rejoins every snapshot it was ever in,
including grace periods that began after it left.  We give the witness.  What
makes it worth having tried is where the failure shows up: not at ReadEnd, which
the change was about, but at ReadBegin, which it did not touch.  One repair is
to make a snapshot a disjoint union of per-thread tickets, so a reader owns its
own membership and spends it; re-entry cannot restore a spent ticket.

We think there is a better one, and we have worked it out.  Stop naming threads
in snapshots.  Let a registration record *when* the reader registered and a
snapshot record *when* the node was unlinked, so that a snapshot's members are
derived -- the readers that registered no later than its grace period began --
rather than stored.  This is what an implementation's grace-period counter does,
which is the mechanism we already describe when comparing with Tassarotti's QSBR
verification; the question was what it is worth as a model, and it is worth
three things.  ReadEnd becomes a write to the departing thread's own
registration and nothing else, and that single write produces all three updates
the published rule performs separately: the thread leaves the readers, leaves
the bounding threads, and leaves every snapshot.  The last of those is an
equation rather than an analogy -- the free list afterwards is literally the
published ReadEnd's free-list update applied to the free list before -- so the
transition our action theorem is stated over is exactly what deleting a
registration produces.  Entering joins nothing, and so leaves the free list
alone, which is the property the published model has to arrange by removing
threads as they leave; and SyncStart's entry is the current reader set, which is
what the published rule says it is.  So all three steps correspond and the only
difference is which of them has to write what.  The bookkeeping also still
satisfies the invariants the snapshot model states about it, so this is a
representation of the same system rather than a different one.

The ownership objection has a second end and the counters answer that too.
Free needs to know a snapshot is empty, and in the published model it knows by
owning the entry -- which is what made the entry the writer's property and the
reader's write illegal to begin with.  Here it owns nothing, because quiescence
past an epoch is stable: once every registration is later than e, every
registration stays later than e, under all four steps, since registrations only
get newer and the counter never goes backwards.  A stable fact needs no
exclusive ownership, so SyncStop hands Free a certificate rather than a
resource, and the free list stops needing to be anybody's property.  The reader
writes only its own registration and the writer reads only a fact it already
holds.

The counters also say exactly where the re-entrancy repair lives: it turns on
the epoch being incremented when the grace period starts, and we give the
contrast -- the same three steps against a SyncStart that stamps without
incrementing, where the returning reader rejoins.  So the increment in an
implementation's counter is not an implementation detail.  It is what
distinguishes a reader's new critical section from its old one, and without it
the model cannot tell them apart.  The cost is a change to the state model,
where the ticket costs only a camera; we took the counters, and the development
is built on them.

That also discharges something we assert of ourselves in the related work,
where we say a variation on our semantics would keep a vector of per-thread
counts in place of the reader set and a snapshot of counts in place of the
bounding set, and that ours is "simpler than this alternative, while also
equivalent".  The alternative is the counter model, and we now give the relation
and the four steps across it: the two states agree on who is reading, who is
bounding, and what the free list is, which are the published model's three
components.  The one worth reading is the guard.  The published rule blocks
until no thread is still bounding; the counters block until every reader
registered no earlier than the current epoch; the theorem is that those are the
same condition.  That is the sentence, proved.

The ghost free list is stamps -- one epoch per node awaiting reclamation, owned
by the writer -- and the threads a snapshot contains are derived from the
registrations, owned one cell each by the readers.  With that, ReadEnd closes as
a triple.  The departing reader hands in its registration and the observation
entries its own cell enumerates and gets its registration back empty; its three
premises are that the enumeration has no repeats, that every entry in it is the
thread's, and that it covers the domain the cell records.  The free list is not
mentioned at all.  The invariant supplies the converse -- that the domain covers
every entry of the thread's carrying an observation of its own -- and ReadBegin
establishes it empty on entry, from the iterator invariant we add and the two
unguarded ones.  The three invariants this work adds are what make the read
side's two rules closable, which is a better argument for them than any of the
counterexamples.

Free's side changes with it.  A stamp does not license freeing a node; the
certificate that the grace period past that stamp is complete does.  That
threads through the environment reading as a parameter beside the scope
condition, and freeable now means "stamped at e, with a certificate for e",
which is what the type meant all along, written where a rule can use it.

A fifteenth invariant defect came out of the reader's read, and it is the
fourth of a kind worth naming.  SyncStart takes one snapshot, so every entry it
writes holds the same set; that is a hypothesis of the snapshot step, and it is
what makes free-list-reachability hold with equality rather than mere inclusion
along a chain of detached nodes.  It is also what the reader's read needs, and
there it has to be an invariant.  The obligation is that a reader taking a
reference to a node already on the free list is one of the threads its grace
period waits for.  The reader is at o and follows an edge to z; if z is on the
free list then so is its predecessor o, with a set *containing* z's, and
iterators-in-free-list applied to o says the reader is in o's set.  What is
wanted is that it is in z's, and the inclusion runs the wrong way.  With the
entries equal the two meet and the proof is four lines; nothing in the nineteen
forces them to agree, and we give a state satisfying all of them, and BR
besides, with two entries holding different sets.

The kind is this: a condition the published proof carries as a hypothesis of
one action, which some other rule needs as an invariant.  Three of the four we
add are of it, and all three are SyncStart's or WriteEnd's.  An invariant set
assembled by asking what each action needs locally will miss exactly these,
because locally there is nothing to miss.

A sixteenth came out of the same read, and it is the fifth of the kind.
T-ReadH gives a reader an iterator observation on the node it has just reached,
and if that node were fresh the fresh-not-reader invariant would fail at once.
So the read must know the node it reaches is not fresh, and what ought to supply
that is fresh-reachability: a fresh node has no incoming edge.  It does not
supply it, because it is stated with a stack reference in its hypothesis -- a
thread that *names* a fresh node -- exactly as WriterFresh was before we
unguarded it, and it is unusable for the same reason, since the observation
outlives the variable.  We give a state satisfying all nineteen in which a
detached node points at a fresh one.  The repair is WriterFresh's, and it is
true of every reachable state because a node stops being fresh the moment it is
published.

That we unguarded WriterFresh, then had to unguard the unlinking invariant, and
now fresh-reachability, is the same pattern three times: each was provable
locally because of its guard and useless outside the critical section because of
it.

That is twenty in all: sixteen invariants, two rule defects, and two defects in
the model.  Sixteen of the twenty were found by attempting the proofs rather than by
reading, and seven of the sixteen are cases whose proof the technical report
calls trivial.  We think
that is the most useful thing the exercise produced: the defects that mattered
were not the ones that looked doubtful. Every defect has
a machine-checked proof or counterexample, the corrected set is machine-checked
satisfiable, and the development has no axioms. The corrected statements now
appear as an appendix, with the published ones retained alongside for
comparison.

The development also now takes every atomic action end to end, rather than one
invariant at a time: the six RCU primitives -- ReadBegin, ReadEnd, SyncStart,
SyncStop, WriteBegin, WriteEnd -- and every type rule that changes the state:
T-Root, T-ReadS, T-ReadH, T-Alloc, T-WriteFH, T-UnlinkH, T-Replace, T-Insert,
T-LinkF-Null and T-Free.  Fifteen theorems, the three variable-binding rules
sharing one and a reader's read composing two.  The structural and subtyping
rules change no state and so carry no action lemma.  Doing the actions whole was
worth more than the sum of the cases.

We should be exact about what these theorems are, since their shape invites
overreading.  Each says an action preserves WellFormed: the global invariants
survive every atomic step.  Axiom soundness asks for more -- the post-state must
satisfy the denotation of the post-type environment, not just the invariants, so
that after T-Replace the fresh reference really is an rcuItr at the replaced
node's path and the replaced one really is unlinked.  That is a separate half,
and it is done too; we come back to it below, because the shape of what is left
over there is worth stating precisely.  What the invariants give is the half of
each case that is about the shared structure rather than the types, which is the
half the technical report treats as trivial and the half where we found the
defects.

For Free, eighteen of the nineteen follow from a single observation, that
freeing removes edges and therefore removes paths, so every invariant with a
reachability or edge hypothesis gets weaker and the nine that do not mention the
heap are untouched outright.  Heap-Domain-Closure is the only one whose
conclusion is about the heap and the only one with any content -- and it needs
the corrected statement, since under the published one the theorem is false.
That is a fair account of what "trivial" was doing: right about eighteen
nineteenths of the case and wrong about the part that mattered.

Three of the five heap mutations are the hardest -- T-Insert, T-UnlinkH and
T-Replace -- and for a reason that appears only when the action is attempted in
full: each writes a field *and* changes the
writer's observations, so ten of the eleven heap-free invariants, which were
identities for the other three actions, acquire content.  The revocation an unlink
performs is of *the writer's* iterator observation and of nothing else -- the
readers still traversing the node keep theirs, which is the whole point of the
structure -- so an update replacing a location's observation set outright would
be wrong, and wrong in a way Fresh-Points-Iterator cannot see and
Reader-Writer-Observation can.  Two earlier repairs are spent here: the premise
excluding a rcuFresh predecessor of the unlinked node, twice in each unlinking
rule, and Fresh-Not-Reader's new conjunct again in the Writer-Unlink case of the
two rules that link a fresh node.

ReadEnd changed its own statement twice, and neither change is visible from any
single invariant.  It needs the departing reader's variables to leave scope in
the same step, or Reader-Writer-Observation fails immediately -- the block
structure of the read critical section supplies this, but only once it is
carried into the action's statement.  And for a time it could not be described as "the
departing thread's entries are gone", which is the natural description and is
correct for every invariant about a thread's observations: the root observation
is anonymous, the observation map was keyed by observer, so that description
deleted the root observation and the second half of Unique-Root failed.  We
gave a well-formed state where it did.  This was an encoding mismatch rather
than a defect in anything published, and it is now gone: with the root
observation read off the root pointer, no thread holds it, the natural
description is the right one, and ReadEnd deletes its keys.

Readers-Iterators-Only turns out to be load-bearing in exactly one place --
ReadEnd, where it is what says the departing thread held no detaching
observation, so that dropping its entries is safe.  It is needed nowhere else
except to preserve itself.  That it is contradictory as published, in exactly
the states where it is needed, is therefore not a cosmetic defect.

One further thing about ReadEnd and WriteEnd, which we report against ourselves
because it is the failure mode a proof assistant does not catch.  Both rules
require *every* variable of the departing thread to leave scope.  Our bridge
from the abstract logical state to the mechanized one represented scope as a
finite set of variable/thread pairs that are out of scope, and with variables
drawn from an infinite supply no finite set contains every variable of a
thread.  The hypothesis was unsatisfiable, so the two theorems that carried it
-- proved, checked, axiom-free -- established nothing.  Flipping the polarity
does not help, since the initial state has nothing out of scope.  Scope is now
a predicate rather than a set, which costs nothing, and both theorems mean what
they say.  We mention it because a vacuous theorem type-checks, closes under
the global context and reports no axioms; only using it finds the problem, and
what found this one was restating the two actions against the Iris invariant
described next.

We also checked the framing property the Views Framework requires, and it does
not hold as published.  This is a defect in the model rather than in an
invariant.  Soundness there is stated modulo composition with an
arbitrary frame, so the invariants must be closed under the composition of
logical states; they are not.  We give two well-formed states whose composition
violates No-Sharing, and the reason is structural rather than incidental: every
state carries the whole heap, so composition must reconcile two copies of it,
and an invariant that constrains two pieces of split per-thread state at once
cannot survive that.  Sharing the heap and adding locality conditions repairs
it, and we give the repaired operation together with a proof that the
invariants are closed under it and a check that the repair is not vacuous.  We
also record what is *not* a defect, since we first thought it was: interference
is declared as a subset of the state space, so closure under it is a typing
obligation rather than a proof obligation.  Composition is a function, and that
is where the real obligation lives.

The repair scales badly to more than one writer -- every write must be absorbed
by every frame, and the rely degenerates -- so we also carried out the
alternative, which is to put the heap in an Iris invariant and leave only the
observation map and free list in the separating part.  All fifteen atomic
actions are now restated in that setting, and the outcome is the useful part:
the pure action lemmas plug in unchanged.  Each restated action is its action
lemma applied once, plus a ghost update.  Ten of those updates are at a single
key; the other five are map-wide, and what they need turned out to be neither a
new camera nor a new frame-preserving update but the observation that a
map-wide change is an iterated single-key one, legal exactly when the thread
owns every fragment it touches -- or the key being free, which is the other way
to earn the right to write there.  Two small induction lemmas supply it, four
lines each.  What they do *not* ask is worth noticing: that the intermediate
maps satisfy WellFormed.  The fold happens inside one opening of the invariant,
so the half-finished states never appear in it and only the endpoint must be
well formed.  That is what lets SyncStop recolour every observation in a single
step.

We then chose the physical-state predicate, which is what turns these steps
into Hoare triples, and that is where the state model gave way.  The standard
choice is a heap of points-to assertions, so that a thread's right to write a
field is a resource.  It was not available: allocation as published gives the
new node *every* field, and field names are drawn from an infinite supply, so
one allocation puts infinitely many cells in the heap.  No finite map
represents such a heap, so there is no camera of the usual shape over it -- they
are all built on finite maps -- and without points-to, a rule whose premise
mentions the heap cannot be stated outside the invariant at all, because the
heap is bound inside it.  We give the proof, stated about the published
definition.

This is the second defect in the model rather than in an invariant, and the
repair is a class declaration: allocation
takes the node's field list and initialises exactly those fields.  The heap is
then finite if it starts finite, which we prove for all three heap-changing
operations.  The cost is local -- allocation gains an argument, heap-domain
closure is unchanged, and the allocation case gains one hypothesis, that the
field list holds every RCU field, which is what makes it a declaration rather
than an arbitrary subset and what the fresh node's denotation needs.  We have
made this change.

With a finite heap, eight actions close completely: Free, all five heap
mutations, T-Alloc, and the binding rules -- which is to say the entire write
side.  ReadBegin and ReadEnd join them once the free list stops being anybody's
exclusive property, which is the epoch representation below, so ten in all.
Each is stated with no parameters and nothing assumed about shared state: the
invariant is opened, every premise is *derived*, the step is taken, the
invariant is closed.

There are four kinds of premise and each is discharged differently.
Observational ones come from the thread's own ghost fragment -- exclusive
control of its entry at a node, and agreement turns that into "the node is
observed freeable", which is the side condition becoming a resource.  Physical
ones come from points-to, the lock token and the stack fragment, none of which
existed before the field list made the heap finite.  Path premises come from
owning the cells along the path; what that derivation does *not* use is the
point -- no invariant, no uniqueness, no shape condition -- because reachability
by a *named* path is a local fact about the cells on it.

Negative premises, that something is absent, cannot come from a fragment at all,
since a fragment says what is and never what is not.  They come from invariants.
"No free-list entry here" is the free-list conjunct we add, with Writer-Unlink
or, for a fresh node, Fresh-Not-Reader.  "This fresh node is unreachable" is
Fresh-Reachable with the pinned root.  "No other thread observes this node as
fresh" is the repaired WriterFresh.  "Every detaching observation is the
writer's" and "this node is not detached" are Writer-Unlink with
Fresh-Not-Reader.  We think that is a concrete answer to what the global
invariants are *for* in a separation-logic reading of this system: they are
exactly the facts a resource cannot carry -- and three of the five just named
are invariants this work adds.

Two premises are of no such kind.  The aliasing premise the unlinking rules
carry -- no rcuFresh reference anywhere points at the node being unlinked -- is
negative and is not an invariant.  What discharges it in the type system is the
environment itself, whose denotation is an intersection over all of it.  Here
the invariant records that the fresh nodes are *enumerated*, the thread holds
the enumeration and those nodes' cells, and the premise becomes a statement
about values the thread owns -- exactly what our checker's noFreshPointsAt
decides by walking the environment.  The quantifier does not disappear; it moves
from the shared state, where nothing could discharge it, to the thread's own
resources, where the type system already had it.

T-Alloc's six premises say the new location is unallocated, unreferenced,
pointed at by nothing, unobserved, unlisted and not the root.  Those look as
though they need an operational semantics -- something must *produce* a fresh
address -- and we expected to have to stop there.  They do not.  An allocator is
a function of the state, and the state is now finite in every component, so the
locations used for anything form a finite set and a location outside it
satisfies all six at once.  The field-list repair pays twice: it made the heap
representable, and it made the allocator constructible.

We then did the other half, the type environment as a resource, and the start
of it produced one thing worth reporting.  The obvious reading, one
resource per variable, does not work: two iterators may name the same node and
two paths overlap in their prefix, so a per-variable reading would demand two
exclusive fragments for one location and two owners for one cell.  The aliasing
side conditions are exactly what rule that out, but they are conditions on the
*environment* rather than on the individual variable, so a per-variable reading
cannot see them.

What works is to own the resources *once*, as maps -- the thread's stack
entries, its observations, its free-list entries, and a set of cells -- and make
each variable's requirement a pure condition on those maps.  A path is then a
fold of lookups in the cell map, an alias is two variables reading the same
entry, and nothing is duplicated because a map has one entry per key.  We prove
that what the writer holds implies the denotation of its type environment, for
every type the write side produces, and that this reading survives an
allocation.  A consequence worth recording: no fractional permissions are needed
on the write side, because overlapping paths share cells by being the same entry
rather than by splitting ownership.  The read side still needs them, for the
different reason that a reader and a writer must hold the same cell at once.

We then stated one rule that way end to end: T-Alloc with a type environment on
both sides, so the thread hands in the resources its environment reads as Gamma
and gets back the resources its environment reads as Gamma with the allocated
variable rebound to rcuFresh.  Nothing new is proved in it -- the step is the
closed triple's and the environment's survival is the monotonicity lemma -- but
it is the shape all fifteen should have, and the first of them to have it.

Every other rule leaves the maps' domains alone, overwriting an entry rather
than growing them at a new location, so what they need is framing rather than
monotonicity: the environment
survives a step whose footprint it does not read.  There are only three kinds of
footprint -- a cell, a location's observations, a stack slot -- so there are
three lemmas rather than seven.  The cell case is where the aliasing side
conditions land: a path is a fold of lookups, so the cells it reads are a
concrete list, and a write outside that list cannot change the fold.  Its
hypothesis is exactly the question our MayAlias procedure decides.  The decision
procedure and the framing lemma are the same condition, one decided and one
proved, which is the tightest connection between the two halves of this work
that we have.

What is left for each rule is the variable it *touches*, and that needed one
thing the reading did not yet have: field maps.  Restricting types to empty
field maps made three of the post-types inexpressible -- T-WriteFH produces a
fresh node with a field written, and T-Insert and T-Replace take field maps as
premises -- so the reading now carries them, read on the thread's own maps in
the same way as everything else.  That also completes the framing lemmas'
hypotheses: a step must not touch a tracked field either.

With that, the touched variable is not a lemma but the definition supplied.  The
three cases that occur -- demotion to unlinked, to freeable, and promotion of a
fresh node to an iterator one field beyond its parent -- are in the development
so the claim is checkable rather than asserted.  Only the promotion has any
content, and the content is that the new path is the old one extended.

Making the rules compose with that reading removed the last two ad-hoc
resources: the fresh nodes had been a separate assertion, and the paths had been
one assertion per iterator.  Both are now conditions on the single cell map the
writer owns.  The path case is the instructive one -- one assertion per iterator
cannot be right, because two iterators' paths overlap in their prefix and a
points-to is exclusive.  All five mutation rules are now stated with their paths
and their fresh nodes as pure facts about that map, which is the same form the
environment reading uses, so the two sides of a rule are written in the same
language.

With that, a mutation goes end to end.  T-LinkF-Null is now stated with a type
environment on both sides: the variables the step does not touch are framed
across it by the two framing lemmas, and the one it does touch gets its new
type.  The only work in the touched case is that the fresh node's new path is
the parent's extended by the field just written, and that its chain of iterator
observations is the parent's chain plus one step.  None of that is about RCU --
it is the bookkeeping that connects a rule's resources to a rule's types, it is
the same bookkeeping for all five mutations, and having done it once the
remaining four are a transcription.

T-UnlinkH follows, and it is the transcription the first one was supposed to
make possible.  It touches three variables rather than one -- the parent's field
map is retargeted, the victim is demoted to unlinked, and the node below is
promoted to the parent's field -- and each is one of three small toolkit
lemmas, with the rest of the environment framed by the same two lemmas in the
same order.  It took no new ideas, which is what the toolkit was for.

T-WriteFH, T-Insert and T-Replace follow without incident, and with them every
heap mutation and the allocation are stated with the environment denotation as
their conclusion.  We should be exact about the other side, since the six are
not uniform there: T-Alloc and T-LinkF-Null take the whole of the environment as
a hypothesis, while the other four take its untouched part together with the
components -- stack entry, observation, path -- that it supplies for each
touched variable.  Those components are exactly what the touched variable's type
unfolds to, so nothing is assumed that the environment does not give, but the
hypothesis is the environment unpacked rather than the environment itself.

The pattern is otherwise uniform: the untouched variables are framed by the two
lemmas, and the touched ones are supplied, each being one of three forms.
T-WriteFH is the smallest, changing no observation at all; T-Insert touches
three variables, the last of which moves one step further down the structure;
T-Replace is a promotion and a demotion at once.  That the last three needed no
new machinery is the substance of the claim: what varies between the rules is
which of the three touched-variable forms occur and how many times, and what
does not vary is anything about RCU.

Three more rules are stated that way now, and two of them needed framing lemmas
the five mutations did not.  T-Free is the only action that makes the thread
*give resources up* rather than write them, so it is the only place the cell map
has to come apart -- into the reclaimed node's cells, which the step consumes,
and the rest, which comes back with the environment.  That also makes the path
condition the harder direction of the two: a write outside a path cannot change
the fold, but a deletion outside it might make the fold fail rather than return
something else, so the invariance is a separate lemma.  Its post-environment
drops the freed variable rather than retyping it to undef -- not an evasion,
because undef's denotation asks for a fact about a node the thread no longer
references, so its honest resource reading is no resource at all, and what undef
means is the pure lemma, where it belongs.

The binding rules needed the mirror image.  The framing lemma we had frames an
observation write at a node the environment does not read, and a bind writes at
the node it does; but every condition a type puts on an entry is a membership,
so an entry that only grows carries all of them.  With that, T-ReadS and T-Root
are one application each.

The reader's read is the ninth, and it is the one that is not a triple.  The
reader's types are read on a smaller condition than the writer's -- no paths, no
cells -- and that condition survives the read for the same reason the binding
rules work: the stack write is at the rebound variable's slot and the
observation write only grows the entry at the node reached, so nothing has to be
assumed about aliasing between the environment and the node read.  What remains
a hypothesis is the edge itself, for the fractional-heap reason above.

The last six are the protocol, and their environments are the smallest in the
development.  ReadBegin and ReadEnd have environments the block structure fixes
rather than derives, so what is worth proving is that the two fit: entering, the
reader gets a registration whose domain is the domain of its empty observation
map, which is exactly the shape the read consumes; leaving, it hands back
exactly the entries that domain names.  We also state the two composed, which
makes re-entry a fact about resources rather than an argument.

SyncStart touches no observation, so the environment is unchanged and all that
is needed is that a free list keeping the entries it had carries it -- the free
list is read by exactly one type.  SyncStop touches no free-list entry, so the
environment changes instead: every variable typed unlinked becomes freeable, and
every other type asks for an observation the recolouring fixes.  WriteBegin's
half is one lookup, and it is one lookup because of the root observation: the
only type the writer starts with is rcuRoot, which is now a stack binding and
nothing else, where under the old encoding it would have asked for the entry at
the root to hold the root observation -- precisely the entry WriteBegin's bulk
update overwrites.  WriteEnd's is empty, for the same reason ReadEnd's is.

Each typed rule was proved in isolation, and nothing had forced one rule's
postcondition to *be* the next one's precondition -- which is the class of
defect that only appears under composition.  So we ran the write side of the
binary search tree delete, the allocation and the splice, through the rules as a
chain over a concrete four-node tree.

It found one immediately.  T-Alloc derives four facts about the location it
invents -- that it is not the root, and that none of the caller's three maps
already mentions it -- and exported none of them.  In isolation that is
invisible, because the lemma's own conclusion does not need them; under
composition it is fatal, because the environment comes back stated over the
extended maps and without those facts no lookup in them can be discharged, so
the next rule cannot start.  Exporting them is the repair, and it is why a rule
that invents a name differs in kind from one that does not.  The other five
invent nothing -- their output maps are the caller's own, extended at keys the
caller supplied -- so allocation was the only rule that could have this defect.

The chain then found something sharper, and it is not about the mechanization.
Three of the four steps compose: the allocation and the two writes that build
the replacement.  The fourth, T-Replace, cannot join them, and not because of
any interface mismatch -- its hypotheses are *inconsistent* with the
allocation's.  T-Replace is stated with "every field is an RCU field", the
simplification the mutation rules inherit from the report; T-Alloc needs the
class declaration the finite heap forced, that every RCU field is one of the
node's declared fields.  Together those say every field name is declared, and
there are infinitely many field names and two declared ones.  We give the
two-line proof.

So the two rules cannot both apply in one program, and the delete is exactly a
program that needs both.  We had reported this incompatibility as a caveat
alongside the field-list repair; it is better as an obstruction, because it says
the paper's own worked example cannot be typed as things stand.

Tracing where the all-RCU assumption is actually used then said what the repair
is, and it is not what we first guessed.  The mutation proofs reach for it in
six places, and every one of them applies it to an edge already in hand -- they
are proving something about a node that some field points at.  So what they need
is not that every field is an RCU field, but that every field *holding a
reference* is: the heap's reference structure lives in RCU fields, and a scalar
field holds a scalar.

That is a weakening, so every proof that used the old assumption still goes
through, and it is consistent with a class declaration, which is what the worked
example needs.  We have made it.  Two rules now say something they should have
said all along: T-WriteFH and T-LinkF-Null write a field that did not previously
hold a reference, so they must state that the field they write is an RCU field;
the other three inherit it from the edge they are replacing.

With the repair in place the splice is no longer blocked by the rules' premises,
and it is blocked by something else which the chain found in turn -- this time
in our development rather than in the rules.  Replacing the deleted node by the
fresh one re-routes every path that ran through it: the node's left child is
still at the same path afterwards, because the fresh node mirrors what it
replaces, but the *intermediate* node on that path is now the fresh one.  The
iterator type's chain-of-iterators condition is about those intermediate nodes,
so it has to be re-derived rather than framed, and our framing lemma cannot do
that -- we show the condition it needs is not merely unproved but false.  The
splice therefore needs a framing lemma that knows about mirroring, which is what
Mirrors does in the pure layer.  We have now built its counterpart in the
environment reading: a fold that reached a node before reaches its swap
afterwards, because at the one cell the rule rewrote the target moves from the
old node to the new and everywhere else the mirror makes the two
indistinguishable.  It needs one thing the other framing lemmas do not -- that
the replaced node has a single predecessor, which is No-Sharing -- and that is
the first place No-Sharing is used to frame rather than to establish an
invariant.

So the framing story has three shapes rather than two.  A step may leave what
the environment reads alone, which two lemmas cover; or it may move what the
environment reads to somewhere indistinguishable, which is the third.  The
second shape is what makes read-copy-update work at all, so it is fitting that
the framing lemma for it is the one that needs No-Sharing.  T-Replace is stated
against that lemma now, so the splice is blocked at neither end: not by the
rules' premises, which the reference repair fixed, and not by the framing, which
the mirror lemma supplies.  With both repairs in place the four steps compose:
the allocation, the two writes that build the replacement, and the splice run as
a single chain over the concrete tree, from the entry environment to one in
which the deleted node is typed unlinked and the fresh node stands in its place.
The write half of the paper's worked example is mechanized.

The other example, the linked-list delete, is mechanized too, and the reason to
do it was different.  It is a single unlinking, so it found nothing the tree had
not; what it checks is that the machinery is about the rules rather than about
trees.  The two differ in what one would want them to differ in -- the tree
needs four steps and a framing lemma about mirroring, the list needs one step
and the ordinary framing -- while the rule applied is the same rule at the same
interface, with nothing list-specific on either side.  That is the reuse claim
we make in the related work, checked rather than asserted.

We tried two larger repairs first, and the wrong ones are instructive.  Guarding
Unlinked-Reachability by RCU-ness so it matches No-Sharing does make the
invariants agree -- but Heap-Domain-Closure needs the same treatment for the
same reason, and the reachability form chains along a path, so the guard becomes
"every field on the path is an RCU field" and propagates into every use.  Both
are repairs to what the invariants *say*.  The defect is not there: it is in the
assumption the proofs make to connect them, and weakening that assumption leaves
every invariant as published.  So this is a second defect in the rules rather
than in an invariant -- the published proofs reconcile two invariants by
assuming a system with no scalar fields at all.

Two smaller things this turned up.  The denotation of freeable was stated as an
equality of *functions*, and in the ghost encoding the free-list entry is a
membership test on a finite set; proving those equal needs functional
extensionality, which we do not assume.  A denotation that cannot be established
without an axiom is a defect in the denotation, and it is stated extensionally
now.  And undef has no resource reading at all: its second conjunct is about the
free list at a node the thread no longer references, which is state it does not
own -- that conjunct belongs in an invariant rather than in a type, and we
report it rather than move it.

What is left is narrower than it was, and one item we listed is not an item at
all.  We said the reader's read could not be stated because the points-to
assertion is exclusive and a reader must read cells the writer also holds, and
that the repair was a fractional heap.  That was wrong.  A reader's iterator is
not a claim about the heap: its denotation is a stack binding, an iterator
observation of the reader's own, and the bounding conjunct -- no path, no cell.
So the reader's environment follows from fragments of the stack and the
observation map and nothing else, which we now state as a lemma with no heap
authority anywhere in it.  What the read does with the heap is consult the
authority inside the invariant to find the successor and carry nothing out,
which needs no fragment and so needs no fraction.  What it does need is the
iterators-in-free-list obligation -- a reader acquiring a reference to a node
already on the free list must be one of the threads its grace period waits for
-- and working out what discharges it is what turned up the fifteenth defect
above.  With the entries agreeing it is four lines.  Both conjuncts are
carried in the invariant now, and the second thing the read wanted was not an
invariant but a decision about the root observation, which we have since taken.
The rule gives the reader an observation at a node it may never have observed,
and to grant one it must hold that node's entry or know the entry absent.  The
reader's cell records which nodes it observes, so the first was always
available for those; the second was not, because ReadEnd left the root
observation behind.  Reading that observation off the root pointer instead lets
ReadEnd delete its keys, makes the clause relating entries to registrations a
statement about entries, and closes the question: a node outside the cell's
domain has no entry of the reader's.  The reader's read is now a step on the
invariant's contents with nothing observational left open.  What is left of it
is the cell read itself: a reader must learn what the field holds, and the
points-to assertion is exclusive.  That is a fractional or snapshot heap -- the
correction above still stands, since neither the reader's environment nor the
step uses a heap fragment, but the read of the cell does.

WriteBegin asks whether the lock is free and SyncStop whether any thread is
still bounding: the two points where the protocol tests or waits on shared state
rather than acting on state it owns.  Both tests are over finite data -- an
option and a finite map -- so each is decidable inside the proof and the attempt
needs no oracle.  We give SyncStop's loop body: it decides whether every
registration still standing is later than the node's stamp, and either takes the
certificate or does not.  The certificate is persistent, because quiescence past
an epoch is stable, and we give what it licenses -- the snapshot is empty, which
is the conjunct freeable asks for.

Termination then reduces further than we expected, and to something that is not
the type system's to prove.  The threads SyncStop waits for are exactly the
snapshot at the node's stamp, a finite set, and each ReadEnd by a member removes
that member and nothing else.  So the wait ends after at most as many ReadEnds
as the snapshot has members, and once they have all ended, in any order, the
test passes; we prove both, and that the last step gives what freeable asks for.
What is left is fairness -- that a reader inside a critical section eventually
leaves it -- which is a property of the client, not of the type system, and
which no invariant of the shared state could supply.  The lock's loop is the
same shape and shorter: it ends when the holder's critical section does.  WriteBegin's test is decidable the same way and is not the obstacle there: it
also takes an iterator observation on every reachable node, which is a bulk
update needing the reachable set enumerated.  That premise is carried
explicitly rather than hidden, and we return to it at the end.

Two more things are new since the last draft, and both are about scope rather
than about a defect.

The first is how much of this is about RCU.  The safety property uses two of the
twenty invariants, and neither of the two theorems mentions a grace period --
which is worth following up, because the iterator observation, "thread t has
announced it may access o", is what a hazard pointer *is*.  We first asked how
much of the system touches reclamation at all, and asked it mechanically: change
the free list to anything and see which invariants survive.  Twenty-one of the
twenty-six conjuncts do, and the proof of each is one tactic -- they are not
preserved by an argument, they are unchanged, because the free list does not
occur in them.  The five that do not survive are exactly the five that mention
it, and one witness state breaks all five.

We then abstracted the reclamation discipline: two components, "t is recorded as
a potential accessor of o" and "o may be reclaimed", and two conditions, that an
observation is recorded and that the guard means nothing is recorded.  Both
safety theorems go through against that interface, and they are the same proofs
with every mention of a free list gone.  RCU is one instance -- the condition at
it is our fourth invariant exactly, and the original theorem falls back out,
which is the check that the abstraction weakened nothing.  Hazard pointers are
the other, and the invariant a hazard-pointer scheme maintains by publishing
before it dereferences is that same invariant with the indices swapped: ours
puts the thread in the node's entry, theirs puts the node in the thread's set.

Two cautions, because that is easy to overstate.  It is the safety argument, not
the whole system: the five free-list invariants do real work elsewhere, and a
hazard-pointer system would need analogues or would do without the rules that
use them.  And what carries over is the argument, not the implementation --
hazard pointers must publish before dereferencing and re-validate after, which
is a memory-ordering obligation we do not model, and the publication invariant
is stated over the logical state, which is exactly what a weak-memory account
would have to earn.

The second is about implementations.  Everything above is about one abstract model.  A client
compiled against a real RCU is safe only if that RCU refines the model, and
refinement proved once over an interface is one theorem where refinement argued
per implementation is one argument per implementation forever.  We now have that
interface: an implementation is a state space with the four protocol actions on
it, reclamation, and three observations -- who is reading, who is bounding, what
the free list says.  The obligation is one clause per action, saying it moves in
step with the published model's, together with three that carry the content:
that the implementation's grace-period guard decides the model's, that it may
free a node only once the node's entry is empty, and that the grace period waits
for exactly the threads now reading.

We read the interface off a proof rather than inventing it.  The counter model
we had already built to settle the equivalence you will find asserted in our
related work came with a relation and one theorem per action; the interface is
that relation with the state made abstract, and the first instance is assembled
from those theorems unchanged.  That the old proof slots into the new interface
without adjustment is the evidence that the interface is the right shape.

The three content clauses are not decoration, and the guard is the easiest one
to show that of.  An implementation can satisfy every step clause and still be
unsafe, because the model's SyncStop does not itself check anything -- the
checking is in when it is allowed to run.  We give the witness: the counter
model with its guard replaced by "always" satisfies every step clause, they
being literally the same theorems, and fails only the guard.  The condition is
the safety property, not tidiness.

The other two are reclamation's, and they are the first fundamental requirement
stated where it bites.  Nothing constrains how an implementation decides that a
grace period is over -- the counter model compares counters, something else may
count quiescent states -- only when the answer may be yes.  One of the two says
when the wait may end and the other says what it was waiting for; together they
are that requirement at this interface.

The transfer then follows.  We pair an implementation state with the observation
bookkeeping, carrying exactly the hypotheses the step relation already had plus
the implementation's guard, and the two halves come from different places: the
implementation supplies the protocol half and the typing derivation supplies the
observation half.  Running the pair, a conforming implementation reaches only
states in which a freeable node has no live reference, and we instantiate that
at the counter model so the chain is visible end to end.

The mutation half comes with it, and the way it does is the point.  The ten heap
actions do not involve the implementation at all -- they are the writer's, and
they touch the heap and the observation map and never the free list or the
reader set -- so the paired relation admits any step of the type system's on the
condition that it leaves the reader set and the bounding set alone, and that
condition holds for all ten by reflexivity, the machine-state transformers being
written to carry both through unchanged.  The paired relation is therefore the
union of the two, a run may interleave the writer's mutations with the protocol
freely, and the safety transfer is about whole programs rather than about the
protocol in isolation.

What we do not claim is that these are the only steps, or that a scheduler
exists producing any particular interleaving.  The relation says what may
happen, not what does; progress and fairness are the same open questions they
were.

What is left is three things, and we would rather name them than let the
invariant work stand in for them.  The third used to be "axiom soundness
proper" and is no longer: all fifteen actions have it.  What is left in its
place is narrower and more specific, and naming it that way seems more useful
than keeping the old heading.

Fairness: that a reader inside a critical section eventually leaves it.  The
wait's termination reduces to that and no further; it is a property of the
client, and no invariant of the shared state could supply it.

A fractional heap for the reader's read.  This is what is left of the read now
that the root observation is derived from the structure rather than stored in a
thread's entry.  A reader must learn what a field holds and the points-to
assertion is exclusive; nothing else about the read is open.  The repair is
standard and it changes the camera under the write side as well, which is why
we name it rather than do it in passing.

The enumeration premises.  Axiom soundness itself is done: every action
preserves WellFormed, that is fifteen theorems and now an induction over runs,
and every action also satisfies the denotation of its post-type environment.
Ten of the fifteen are Hoare triples -- the whole write side, the binding rules,
Free, and the two read-block boundaries.  The other five are the step plus the
environment with a premise made explicit, and those premises are what is left.
They are of exactly two kinds.

The reader's read has one: the edge it follows, because a reader cannot learn a
cell's contents while the points-to assertion is exclusive.  That is the
fractional heap named above.

The other four -- SyncStart, SyncStop, WriteBegin, WriteEnd -- have an
enumeration premise, and it is the same premise four times: they are the bulk
updates, and a bulk update needs its set enumerated.  "Every detached node is
stamped", "every entry of the writer's is recoloured", "every reachable node is
observed", "every entry is given up".  No resource a thread holds discharges
those.  Where it can be stated as a fact about the thread's *own* map rather
than the shared one we state it that way -- the writer holds its whole column --
which is the honest form and is what a writer that has tracked its observations
since WriteBegin actually has.  We would rather name that than dress it up: it
is the shape of what is left, and it is narrower than "six rules are missing".



A third thing about scope, and it is about the memory model.  We are
sequentially consistent; Tassarotti et al. verify under release-acquire, where a
thread's view of the heap is a sub-heap of what has been written -- a thread
sees some of the writes that happened and never one that did not.  So the
question a weak reading puts to an invariant is exactly whether it still holds
when the heap shrinks, and one that does transfers from the union of all views,
which is what the writer maintains, to each thread's own view for free.

We asked it the same way we asked the last one, mechanically.  Sixteen of the
twenty-six conjuncts do not mention the heap at all and survive any change to it
whatever, the proof of each being one tactic; nine mention it only to rule
something out and survive shrinking; exactly one does not.

That one is heap-domain closure.  Every other invariant reads the heap to rule
something out; this one reads it to require something -- that the node at the
end of an edge is in the heap -- and a thread that has acquired the edge but not
the node breaks it.  That is not an artificial state: it is the publication
race, the writer initialising a node and then linking it while a reader acquires
the link without the initialisation, and we give it as a witness.

Read as an obligation, heap-domain closure under a weak memory model *is* the
publish-subscribe guarantee -- the second of the four fundamental requirements,
and the release-acquire pair Tassarotti names first.  So the single place a
per-thread heap bites is the single place the literature says synchronisation is
needed, and it tells us the synchronisation points are not only the protocol's
two but a third at the link, where a writer publishes.  It also says what is not
at risk: the memory-safety argument uses two heap-free invariants, and our
publish-subscribe proof uses one heap-free and one that survives shrinking.

Stating that as an obligation takes one theorem.  A state is a base together
with a view per thread, each a sub-heap of the base; "published" says every
thread that can reach a node can see it; and then nineteen of the twenty
conjuncts transfer from the base to every view with no hypothesis at all, the
twentieth being exactly that condition.  A weak account does not have to
re-prove the invariants per thread -- it has to establish one property at the
link.

The heap is not the only shared state, and the other kind has the opposite
character.  The registrations are shared too, and they are what our relation
ties to the reader set.  For the heap a thread that sees less is safe; for the
registrations a writer that sees fewer than exist concludes a grace period has
ended when it has not, takes the certificate, and frees a node whose snapshot
still contains the reader.  We give that state.  Seeing less is the hazard, not
the safeguard.

That is why the two need synchronisation in different places: the heap at the
write that publishes, the registrations at the read that decides the wait is
over.  The relation's first clause is an iff, and a sub-map view gives one
direction and loses the one the wait depends on, so the weak-memory refinement
obligation is not a weakened form of the sequentially consistent one but the
same obligation with the reads that establish it required to be acquires.

Those are three synchronisation points, and they are the three Tassarotti names:
the link is Release-Acquire-1, the registration write and the scan that reads it
the second and third.  We arrive at the same three from the invariants rather
than from the algorithm, which is the check on all of this.

The model is written too.  It is release-acquire in its view form: a history
per cell, a view as a timestamp per cell, and three steps -- a write that may or
may not release, rcu_assign_pointer being the one that does and a field
initialisation the one that does not, and a read that acquires, which is
rcu_dereference.  Nothing about views is assumed: the bookkeeping and the
closure release-acquire turns on hold at the initial configuration and are
preserved by every step.

One modelling point is worth naming because the obvious simplification is wrong.
The released view belongs to the message, not to the location.  Attach it to the
location and publishing a location twice retroactively changes what an earlier
reader is obliged to have acquired, and the closure is not preserved.

What comes out is that a reader is at least as far along as the publisher of
anything it can see, and so sees what the publisher wrote, with the two
programmer's obligations named rather than buried: initialise before you
publish, and do not rewrite afterwards -- both of which the fresh discipline
enforces on the write side.  That the release is necessary is not argued but
exhibited: the same run twice differing in one bit, where the relaxed version is
a perfectly good run of the semantics in which the reader sees the link and the
node's field as it was before initialisation.

And then the bridge: a run under the discipline reaches a configuration in which
every thread's own view satisfies every invariant, given that the sequentially
consistent state whose heap is what has been written does.  Nineteen of the
twenty conjuncts come free from the partition above; the twentieth is
publication, and it comes from the semantics.

The registrations are carried by the same three steps, a registration being a
cell, and doing so corrected the guess we started with.  A stale generation read
by a reader is safe: it registers at an earlier generation, so it looks older
than it is and a grace period that need not have waited for it waits anyway.  A
stale registration read by the writer is the hazard, and we give that run.  But
reads only move forward -- coherence is built into the read step -- so a scan
that keeps reading advances monotonically and all it needs is to reach the last
message, which is the same fairness assumption we already reduce the wait's
termination to.  Weak memory adds no new obligation to the grace period.

Reclamation is where the memory model stops being the answer.  A view holds only
what was written, and a thread that has caught up past the unlink cannot see the
old link, so reclamation is safe exactly when every thread has caught up.  The
witness is a reader holding a reference to a node nothing reachable points at
any more, with both writes releasing -- the strongest thing the model has.  No
synchronisation helps; only not reclaiming does.

The write-once restriction is lifted, and how is the useful part.  We set out to
show it was necessary by building a reader whose view had never been the heap,
and every attempt failed at the same step: the acquire pulls the reader forward
everywhere, not only at the cell it read.  Under RCU's actual discipline -- one
writer, every write releasing, readers that only read -- a releasing write
publishes the writer's whole knowledge, and with a single writer those
publications are totally ordered, so every reader's view is one of the writer's
past views.  The bridge then needs nothing about which cells are written twice;
it assumes only that the states the writer passed through are well formed, which
is what the rest of the development proves.  Two writers and the publications
stop being a chain; one relaxed write and the reader acquires nothing -- both
outside what ToRCUWrite permits, which is to say the type system is what
enforces that theorem's hypothesis.

What stands is that this is release-acquire and not weaker.  A claim about the
Linux kernel memory model, where rcu_dereference rests on address dependencies
rather than on acquire, would be a different piece of work.

What we claim is narrower than "RCU is verified under weak memory" and, we
think, more useful: the single invariant a weak memory model endangers is
publication, publication is what the releasing write buys, and the buying can be
written down.


One last thing worth saying about the infinite-heap defect, because it cuts the
other way.  The checker never had it.  Fields there are a bitset over a declared
field list, which is the class declaration the repair introduces, and it is what
makes the coverage side condition decidable at all.  The model that had to be
approximated to be executable turned out to be the correct one, and the model
written for the proof was the one that was wrong.  We have added checks to the
prototype recording the correspondence.

> All reviewers: Concerns about complexity, decidability, automation


Our focus in this paper, as Reviewer 1 noted, is the core reasoning principles where we chose a type system to focus on articulating those principles. As well-stated by Reviewer 2: this type system should be general enough to capture interesting programs, and likely requires less knowledge of advanced logical concepts to apply than e.g., an Iris-based logic.


On decidability, Reviewer 2 is right about the system as presented, and we keep
that concession: a declarative derivation may insert T-Conseq anywhere, so the
declarative system is not decidable as stated.  But the question we should have
answered is the one behind it -- can the side conditions be discharged
mechanically -- and there we can now say considerably more than "future work".

Every framing side condition in the type rules is a negated MayAlias query, so
that is where decidability is decided.  On the path language as written the
general problem is one of word equations, because abstracted iteration lets the
same index occur in two places.  We give instead an *algorithmic* presentation:
a fragment of the path language (paths in which no index occurs twice, drawn
from a fixed list in increasing order), a canonical join at control-flow merges,
reindexing-by-matching at back edges, and a decision procedure for MayAlias that
is linear after a prefix reduction and then a product construction.  Subsumption
is applied only at fixed points.  Checking a critical section becomes a
terminating forward dataflow over environments in that fragment, and the
fragment is the class of programs the algorithm is complete for.

We can also characterise its precision exactly, which we think answers the part
of the question that matters for a diagnostic.  MayAlias is sound in the
direction the type system needs -- every use is under a negation, so
over-approximating rejects safe programs but never accepts unsafe ones -- and it
is *exact* whenever no path index survives the prefix reduction in both
arguments.  A syntactic condition guarantees that: all paths mentioning an index
agree on the segments before it, which is what a traversal produces anyway, since
the cursors of a loop are reindexed by the same back edge.  Measured against
brute-force enumeration over 100,000 random pairs from the fragment across five
seeds, no answer was unsound, and the procedure is exact in all 93,564 of the
pairs where no index survives the prefix reduction in both arguments.  The 6,436
that do share one carry the whole of the residual imprecision, and it is
substantial there -- the procedure agrees on 64% of them -- which is the point:
the imprecision is not spread thinly over the domain but concentrated outside
the anchored class.  A second test, over a further 100,000 pairs, is exact on
all 90,256 that are anchored.  The suites are seeded, so these are reproducible
figures rather than a sample.

Reviewer 3's intuition about automation is borne out, and further than we
expected when we drafted this.  There is a checker.

Four layers in dependency-free C++17 -- the path domain, the types and
environments, the type rules as transfer functions, and a statement IR with a
forward dataflow over it -- passing 93 unit and property checks, the latter
comparing each abstract operation against the concrete semantics by
enumeration.  On top of them a Clang front end translates a C function into the
IR through the CFG, taking back edges from the dominator tree and refinements
from the branch conditions, and reports what the dataflow finds against the
source.

It needs no annotation language, and rather less than we first claimed.  __rcu
on a field is enough to recognise the reads and the writes: they are taken from
the field's type, not from an accessor macro, so rcu_dereference and
rcu_assign_pointer need not appear at all.  rcu_read_lock/rcu_read_unlock,
synchronize_rcu and the kfree family distinguish every remaining action the type
system has a rule for; only the root has to be marked, because nothing in the
source distinguishes it.  We think that is the
more useful claim than any figure about annotation burden: the annotations are
already in the code, and what has been missing is a check of what they mean.

The binary search tree delete of the appendix, written as C, type checks --
including the two-child case that copies the successor, splices the copy in and
reclaims the original after a grace period.  We report that together with what
the checker rejects, since a file that passes and cannot be made to fail is not
evidence: the same function with one field left out of the copy is rejected by
the mirroring premise, and with the grace period removed is rejected at the
free.

What it does not do: a function is checked on its own, with its pointer
parameters assumed to be iterators, and summaries carry kinds across a call but
not paths.  The limit shows up in the same example -- the delete checks when it
reads its cursor out of the root and does not when the two cursors arrive as
parameters, because nothing at the boundary says the second is the first's
child.

This is now Appendix B, and the body points to it where MayAlias is introduced.

> R2&R3: Interactions between critical sections and non-critical-section code


The type system ensures unlinked and freeable references are handled *linearly*, as they cannot be dropped (coerced to undef). The top-level rule ToRCUWrite does ensure unlinked references have been freed, by forbidding them in the critical section's post-type-environment, as Reviewer 3 noticed. So the initial environment lacks rcuItr, and "trailing" rcuItr may not escape.

> R2: Delayed Ownership Transfer


We agree this is the most delicate part of the proof, and we can now say
considerably more than we could at submission.

The mechanism is as you describe: once an object is observed as *unlinked* by
the writer in the observation map of the logical state, it waits for all other
threads' *iterator* observations to be dropped (the object becomes *freeable*
for the writer) before being reclaimed.

What makes that safe is the property that a node awaiting reclamation is never
reachable from the root, and this is exactly where the published
Unlinked-Reachability fails. It is stated one step, with hypothesis "unlinked"
and conclusion "unlinked or freeable", so an induction along a path stalls at
the first freeable predecessor, where no invariant applies. The Unlink proof
appeals to it in the reachability form regardless. Closing the hypothesis under
the disjunction repairs it; we have machine-checked both the repair and a
counterexample to the published form, along with the corresponding chaining
property for the free list. Together those two are what keep a newly arrived
reader away from a node scheduled for reclamation.

We have also mechanized the Free case of heap-domain closure, and now the whole
of Free: all nineteen invariants, for the action as a step. Free is the only
action that removes a location, so it is the only place where the obligation --
that no surviving edge points at the freed node -- is real; we give a
counterexample showing it cannot be dispensed with, and the corrected
Heap-Domain-Closure is what discharges it, from the repaired
Unlinked-Reachability. The technical report discharges that case, and the whole
group of memory axioms for that action, as "trivial".

Two further pieces of that argument are now proved rather than asserted.  The
claim that a node awaiting reclamation is not reachable is stated directly, once
for each rule that produces one -- unlinking and replacement -- and it is what
the second half of Unique-Root needs.  That invariant says the writer observes
every reachable node as an iterator, and both rules revoke exactly one iterator
observation, so without the unreachability result it fails at the very step that
revokes it.  Unique-Reachable does not supply this: it says the result is still
a tree, not which nodes are in it, and this is the one place where the
difference matters.  T-Insert, which revokes nothing, needs neither.

And the rest of the protocol is machine-checked as whole actions.  SyncStop is
where the empty bounding set is spent: Readers-In-Free-List says every thread in
an entry bounds a reclamation, and afterwards there are none, so every entry is
empty -- which is exactly the conjunct the *freeable* denotation asks for,
obtained rather than assumed.  All four steps of the reclamation chain --
ReadBegin, ReadEnd, SyncStart and SyncStop -- are complete as actions, each
preserving all nineteen invariants, and ReadBegin and ReadEnd are also closed
triples in the resource reading, for the reasons given above.

It is also worth stating the property all of this exists to establish, because
once the invariants are right it is four lines and uses exactly two of them.  A
variable typed freeable names a node the writer is about to reclaim, and memory
safety needs that reclaiming it pulls the rug from under nobody.
Iterators-in-free-list says a thread observing a node as an iterator is in that
node's entry, and the freeable denotation says the entry is empty because the
grace period has completed; so nobody observes it as an iterator.
Reader-writer-observation says a live variable's referent carries an observation
-- an iterator one, or a detaching one belonging to the lock holder -- so the
first case is now impossible and the second identifies the holder as the writer
itself.  The only live reference to a node about to be freed is the writer's
own.

Both of those are invariants the published set already has, which is the right
outcome: the sixteen we add are not there to prove this, they are there to make
the fifteen actions preserve the set that does.

Said over a single state, that is weaker than memory safety asserts, which is
about runs; the gap is one induction, and we close it: the invariants are inductive
along executions, so at no point in any run from a well-formed start does a
thread other than the writer hold a live reference to a node the writer is about
to reclaim.  The relation is written out, one constructor per action carrying that
action's hypotheses, so its preservation is fifteen applications and nothing
else, and the safety theorem is stated at the whole system rather than at one
action.

Progress deserves a word, because the obvious statement is not the right one.
"Some step is always possible" is false and should be: a state in which no
thread can move is a deadlock, and whether one is reachable is a question about
the program.  Our relation relates states, not configurations, so
relation-level progress is not statable there and would not mean what it sounds
like.  What progress means for this system is per-thread and per-primitive --
that a well-typed thread about to execute a primitive finds its side condition
true -- which is the guards, and which we treat under Alglave's third
requirement.

Two of Alglave et al.'s four fundamental requirements are now theorems about
our model rather than arguments in prose, and which invariants they need is the
point.  The essential clause of their correctness criterion is that reader
critical sections do not span grace periods; a section spans one if it began
before the grace period began and was still running when it completed, and the
grace period for a node completes exactly when every registration still standing
began later than the node's stamp.  So the clause is immediate from that
condition, and the content is in what establishes it -- that the wait ends
because the readers do -- and in what it licenses, which is the reclamation.

The publish-subscribe requirement is the sharper one.  A freshly allocated node
cannot be observed by a reader until it is published, and proving it needs two
invariants: fresh-not-reader, so that a fresh node carries no iterator
observation and no reader observes it, and the unguarded fresh-reachability we
had to add, so that it has no incoming edge and no reader can walk to one.
Without the second the guarantee is half of itself -- a reader could not be
*holding* an unpublished node, but nothing said it could not reach one.  That a
published correctness criterion turns out to need an invariant the published
invariant set does not have is the sharpest form our finding takes.

The third requirement, that the primitives execute unconditionally rather than
failing and retrying, we first wrote off as needing no proof, on the grounds
that the primitives are total functions on the machine state.  That was wrong,
and reading our own semantics again is what shows it.  Five of the six carry a
side condition: ReadBegin is written with tid different from the lock holder and
with the reader set extended by disjoint union, so it requires the thread to be
neither the writer nor already a reader; ReadEnd is written with the disjoint
union on the *left*, so it requires the thread to be a reader; SyncStart
requires an empty bounding set; WriteBegin requires the lock free; SyncStop
requires the bounding set empty.  Only WriteEnd is unguarded.

So there is something to prove, and it is the right thing -- not that the guards
are trivial but that a well-typed thread never finds one false.  For the
reader's two it is a theorem, discharged by the thread's own registration
together with the invariant clause that the lock holder holds none: a thread
with an unregistered cell is neither the writer nor a reader, and a thread with
a registered one is a reader and still not the writer.  The writer's three are
not discharged and we prove they cannot be, by giving a state satisfying every
invariant in which all three are false at once; two of them are the blocking
ones, and blocking rather than failing is what the requirement asks, and
SyncStart's is discharged by the protocol, since Sync is SyncStart followed by
SyncStop.

The fourth, read-to-write upgrade, we do not provide, and it is a performance
optimisation orthogonal to memory safety.

We will expand Section 6 along these lines.
